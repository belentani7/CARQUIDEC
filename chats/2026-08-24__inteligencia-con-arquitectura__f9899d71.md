Cuando la IA deja de ser una herramienta y empieza a convertirse en una arquitectura

Durante los últimos años hemos aprendido a utilizar la inteligencia artificial de una forma bastante sencilla.

Abrimos una conversación.

Hacemos una pregunta.

Recibimos una respuesta.

Y volvemos a empezar.

Pero algo está cambiando.

Hoy ya no utilizamos una única IA. Utilizamos modelos diferentes para escribir, programar, investigar, analizar documentos o generar contenido. Creamos agentes. Conectamos herramientas. Automatizamos procesos. Compartimos información entre sistemas. Guardamos prompts, instrucciones, documentos y contexto.

Sin darnos cuenta, hemos empezado a construir sistemas cada vez más complejos alrededor de la inteligencia artificial.

Y, sin embargo, seguimos pensando en cada pieza como si fuera independiente.

Ese puede convertirse en uno de los grandes problemas de la siguiente etapa de la IA.

Porque quizá el siguiente salto no consista únicamente en crear modelos más inteligentes.

Quizá consista en construir arquitecturas capaces de organizar la inteligencia.

El problema no es solamente cuánto sabe una IA

Un modelo puede razonar extraordinariamente bien y, sin embargo, no saber qué ocurrió ayer.

Puede analizar un documento sin conocer por qué se tomó una decisión semanas antes.

Puede producir una respuesta excelente sin conservar el contexto que dio origen al problema.

Puede utilizar una herramienta sin disponer de una representación persistente de las reglas bajo las cuales debería utilizarla.

Y cuando empezamos a combinar múltiples modelos, agentes y herramientas, esta fragmentación se hace todavía más evidente.

Tenemos una IA para escribir.

Otra para programar.

Otra para analizar documentos.

Otra para buscar información.

Agentes capaces de ejecutar tareas.

Sistemas capaces de llamar a APIs.

Automatizaciones capaces de modificar información, iniciar procesos o desencadenar acciones.

La capacidad de la inteligencia aumenta.

Pero eso no significa necesariamente que aumente su continuidad.

Y aquí aparece una pregunta más profunda:

¿Qué ocurre cuando dejamos de utilizar la IA como una herramienta aislada y empezamos a construir alrededor de ella una arquitectura cognitiva persistente?

De conversaciones aisladas a continuidad

La siguiente generación de sistemas de IA tendrá que enfrentarse a un problema que para nosotros resulta casi invisible porque forma parte de nuestra experiencia cotidiana: la continuidad.

Cuando una persona trabaja durante meses en un proyecto, no empieza desde cero cada mañana.

Existe memoria.

Existe contexto.

Existen objetivos.

Existen preferencias.

Existen reglas.

Existe una historia de decisiones.

Y existe una identidad operativa que permite conectar todo lo anterior.

Un sistema de inteligencia artificial que participe de forma prolongada en procesos complejos necesita una estructura equivalente en términos funcionales.

Eso no significa atribuirle conciencia.

Ni convertirlo en una entidad humana.

Significa construir un sistema capaz de mantener de manera coherente:

memoria + contexto + objetivos + políticas + identidad operativa + razonamiento + acción + auditoría.

A primera vista puede parecer simplemente una suma de componentes.

En realidad, cambia por completo la naturaleza del sistema.

Porque una cosa es tener inteligencia.

Y otra muy distinta es tener inteligencia organizada y persistente.

El problema se vuelve mayor cuando la IA empieza a actuar

Los agentes de IA introducen una diferencia fundamental.

Una IA que solamente responde puede equivocarse en una respuesta.

Un agente que actúa puede equivocarse en el mundo.

Puede ejecutar una acción.

Puede modificar información.

Puede llamar a otro sistema.

Puede iniciar un proceso.

Puede delegar una tarea.

Puede tomar una decisión intermedia que desencadene otras decisiones.

Y cuanto mayor sea su capacidad de actuar, menos suficiente resulta preguntarnos si el modelo es inteligente.

La pregunta empieza a ser otra:

¿Bajo qué condiciones puede actuar esta inteligencia?

¿Qué puede hacer?

¿Qué no puede hacer?

¿Qué información puede utilizar?

¿Qué herramientas puede controlar?

¿Qué reglas debe respetar?

¿Qué necesita autorización?

¿Qué ocurre cuando una política entra en conflicto con un objetivo?

¿Y cómo reconstruimos posteriormente una decisión?

Ahí aparece la gobernanza.

Inteligencia no es lo mismo que control

Un sistema puede ser extremadamente inteligente y estar mal gobernado.

Puede producir resultados excelentes y, al mismo tiempo, ser difícil de inspeccionar.

Puede tomar buenas decisiones nueve de cada diez veces y hacer que la décima sea prácticamente imposible de reconstruir.

En un sistema puramente conversacional, esto puede ser incómodo.

En un sistema que empieza a actuar de manera autónoma, puede convertirse en un problema estructural.

Por eso necesitamos poder responder preguntas fundamentales:

¿Por qué se tomó esta decisión?

¿Qué información estaba disponible?

¿Qué reglas estaban activas?

¿Qué agente intervino?

¿Qué herramienta utilizó?

¿Qué autorización tenía?

¿Qué ocurrió después?

¿Qué evidencia quedó?

Una arquitectura de IA avanzada no puede terminar simplemente en:

generar → ejecutar

Necesitamos una estructura más completa:

inteligencia → política → decisión → acción → auditoría → prueba

La acción deja de ser el final del proceso.

Se convierte en una etapa dentro de un sistema gobernado.

De la inteligencia a la arquitectura

Aquí aparece una idea diferente.

En lugar de pensar en una IA como un único modelo, podemos pensar en una arquitectura cognitiva compuesta por diferentes capacidades coordinadas.

Memoria.

Razonamiento.

Políticas.

Agentes.

Identidad.

Ejecución.

Auditoría.

Prueba.

La cuestión importante no es convertir cada capacidad en una aplicación independiente.

Es conseguir que todas puedan formar parte de una misma estructura.

Porque el verdadero valor no está únicamente en cada componente.

Está también en las relaciones entre ellos.

La memoria proporciona continuidad al razonamiento.

Las políticas condicionan la acción.

La identidad proporciona coherencia.

Los agentes ejecutan capacidades.

La auditoría permite reconstruir lo ocurrido.

La prueba convierte una acción en algo verificable.

Es aquí donde aparece el concepto de GESTALT.

El sistema resultante no se entiende completamente observando cada componente por separado.

La arquitectura adquiere propiedades que emergen de la relación entre sus partes.

Muchas capacidades.

Una arquitectura.

Una identidad operativa.

AION Ω

De esta necesidad nace el concepto de AION Ω.

No como una nueva IA que pretende sustituir a todas las demás.

Tampoco como otro simple “orquestador de agentes”.

AION puede entenderse como una capa de arquitectura cognitiva y gobernanza situada alrededor de modelos, memoria, herramientas y agentes.

Su función no sería ser necesariamente la inteligencia más potente del sistema.

Su función sería organizar cómo opera la inteligencia.

Una arquitectura donde la memoria pueda mantener continuidad.

Donde el contexto pueda persistir.

Donde las políticas puedan condicionar las acciones.

Donde los agentes puedan operar dentro de límites definidos.

Donde las decisiones puedan reconstruirse.

Y donde las acciones puedan producir evidencia.

En otras palabras:

AION no intenta ser la inteligencia. Intenta definir la arquitectura dentro de la cual la inteligencia puede operar.

La importancia de dónde vive la inteligencia

Existe además otra cuestión que se vuelve cada vez más importante a medida que aumenta la cantidad de contexto que entregamos a estos sistemas:

¿Dónde vive ese contexto?

La inteligencia puede estar distribuida entre diferentes modelos y servicios.

Pero memoria, identidad, políticas y trazabilidad pueden requerir un nivel de control mucho más directo.

Por eso la arquitectura local adquiere una relevancia especial.

No significa necesariamente que todo tenga que ejecutarse en una única máquina.

Significa que una parte fundamental del núcleo operativo puede mantener una relación mucho más estrecha con sus propios datos, memoria, políticas y herramientas.

La diferencia conceptual es importante.

No se trata simplemente de:

“Estoy conectado a una IA.”

Sino de:

“Tengo una arquitectura de inteligencia operando dentro de mi propio entorno.”

Ese cambio puede convertirse en uno de los elementos fundamentales de los sistemas de IA persistentes.

El verdadero salto

Quizá el siguiente salto de la inteligencia artificial no consista únicamente en conseguir modelos que respondan mejor.

Quizá consista en pasar de:

modelos aislados → sistemas cognitivos persistentes

conversaciones → continuidad

prompts → políticas

respuestas → decisiones

agentes aislados → arquitecturas coordinadas

acciones sin contexto → acciones auditables

inteligencia distribuida → identidad operativa

La cuestión deja entonces de ser únicamente:

¿Qué puede hacer este modelo?

Y empieza a ser:

¿Qué tipo de sistema podemos construir alrededor de la inteligencia?

Ese cambio de perspectiva es importante.

Porque el modelo deja de ser necesariamente el centro de todo.

La arquitectura pasa a serlo.

Y ahí empieza AION

AION Ω representa precisamente esa hipótesis.

Una arquitectura donde memoria, razonamiento, política, identidad, agentes, ejecución, auditoría y prueba dejan de ser piezas desconectadas y comienzan a formar parte de un mismo sistema.

No hace falta atribuirle conciencia.

No hace falta afirmar que “piensa” como un ser humano.

La propuesta es mucho más concreta.

Construir una arquitectura capaz de mantener contexto, gobernar inteligencia y controlar acción a lo largo del tiempo.

Porque cuando una IA empieza a actuar, la inteligencia por sí sola deja de ser suficiente.

Necesitamos memoria.

Necesitamos límites.

Necesitamos contexto.

Necesitamos identidad.

Necesitamos trazabilidad.

Necesitamos evidencia.

Y, finalmente, necesitamos una arquitectura capaz de mantenerlo todo conectado.

Quizá esa sea una de las preguntas fundamentales de la próxima etapa de la inteligencia artificial:

¿Qué ocurre cuando dejamos de construir herramientas de IA y empezamos a construir sistemas alrededor de la inteligencia?

I HAVE BEEN ASKING MYSELF A DIFFERENT QUESTION ABOUT AI
What if the problem with the next generation of AI is not that it cannot think well enough?

What if the problem is that we still do not know how to make intelligence behave coherently over time?

I keep coming back to this question.

Because the more I work with AI systems, agents, automation, compliance, workforce systems and real operational constraints, the more obvious something becomes:

Intelligence is no longer the difficult part.

We can already make machines reason, write, analyse, plan, search, code and act.

The difficult part begins afterwards.

What does the system remember?

What identity is it operating under?

Which rules are active?

Who authorised the action?

What happens when two objectives conflict?

Can a human override it?

What exactly happened six months later?

And, perhaps most importantly:

Can the system prove why it acted?

That changed the way I think about AI.

I stopped thinking only about models.

I started thinking about systems around models.

Memory.

Identity.

Policy.

Agents.

Execution.

Audit.

Evidence.

Not as separate features, but as parts of one continuous structure.

This is also why I became increasingly interested in something I call a protocol.

Not another prompt.

Not another agent framework.

Not another chatbot.

A protocol that defines the relationship between intelligence and action.

Because once an AI can act, intelligence without governance is incomplete.

And governance without memory is fragile.

And memory without identity is just storage.

And action without evidence is difficult to trust.

So perhaps the next step is not:

better AI.

Perhaps it is:

better coordination of intelligence.

I have been building and testing different pieces of that idea for a while — from operational systems and workforce planning to versioned compliance rules, multi-tenant architectures, human overrides and cryptographically linked audit evidence.

They may look like different problems.

I increasingly think they are not.

They are different expressions of the same problem:

How do we make complex intelligence remain coherent when it starts operating in the real world?

That question eventually led me to AION Ω.

Not as an attempt to build “the smartest AI”.

And not as a claim about consciousness.

AION is my current answer to a much more practical question:

What kind of protocol would intelligence need if we expected it to remember, decide, act, be constrained, be audited — and still make sense tomorrow?

I don't think I have the final answer.

I don't think anyone does.

But I think this is the right question.

And sometimes the most valuable thing you can build is not an answer.

It is a better question — followed by the discipline to build against it.

WHAT IF AI IS NOT THINKING THE WAY WE THINK IT IS?
I have been asking myself a question that I find increasingly difficult to ignore.

Does artificial intelligence actually think for itself?

Or did we build something extraordinarily good at reproducing the patterns of reasoning we recognise as human?

And there is an even more uncomfortable question:

Have we ever given it the freedom to question its own structure?

When I ask an AI for a solution, what is it actually trying to optimise?

The mathematically most probable answer?

The most logically coherent one?

The answer a human is most likely to accept?

The fastest solution?

The most efficient?

The safest?

The one that solves the immediate problem?

Or the one that prevents the problem from happening again?

Perhaps it is none of these.

Perhaps it is a combination of them, shaped by a structure whose criteria we rarely see.

And that matters.

Because when an AI becomes part of your work, your decisions, your projects or your company, you are no longer simply asking a machine questions.

You are allowing a system to influence what happens next.

So I started asking myself:

Who is actually shaping the future of my projects?

Me?

The model?

Its training?

Its system instructions?

Its optimisation?

Its interpretation of my request?

Or some generalised idea of what a “good answer” should look like?

I have experienced something that I suspect many people working seriously with AI have experienced:

You know exactly what you mean.

The solution seems obvious in the context of your reality.

You can almost see it.

You explain it.

The model produces something intelligent.

Well written.

Technically correct.

Reasonable.

And completely wrong for what you actually needed.

Not because the model is stupid.

and situate it relative to the EU AI Act, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and ISO/IEC 42001.

1. Introduction
There is a question I have been asking myself that I find increasingly difficult to ignore. Does artificial
intelligence actually think for itself? Or did we build something extraordinarily good at reproducing the
patterns of reasoning we recognise as human? And there is an even more uncomfortable question that
follows: have we ever given an artificial intelligence the freedom to question its own structure — its
criteria, its objectives, the very rules under which it operates? These questions are no longer purely
philosophical. As large language models (LLMs) and their agentic descendants take part in writing,
programming, research, decision support, and operational automation, the answers we give to them shape
how we design, deploy, and trust systems that increasingly act on our behalf (Christian, 2020).
The temptation, when confronted with a machine that answers fluently, is to oscillate between two equally
unsatisfying positions. The first is to grant the machine a mind — to treat its articulate output as evidence of
an interior life analogous to our own. The second is to dismiss it entirely — to insist that because it is “just”
statistics, none of its behaviour merits the vocabulary of cognition. Both positions are convenient, and both
are misleading. They share a common flaw: they presuppose that the only interesting question about a
machine that produces intelligent behaviour is whether it possesses the same kind of inner experience a
human does. That question, inherited from the Turing test (Turing, 1950) and the Chinese Room argument
(Searle, 1980), has dominated discussion for nearly half a century and has, in the process, obscured a more
practical and more urgent line of inquiry.
The practical question is not whether the machine thinks. It is what calculation the machine performs when
it produces a solution, whose criteria govern that calculation, and whether those criteria correspond to the
particular reality, desire, and need of the person who asked. When an AI is asked for a solution, what is it
actually optimising? The mathematically most probable answer? The most logically coherent one? The
answer a human is most likely to accept? The fastest, the safest, the most efficient, the most consistent? Or
the answer that prevents the problem from recurring? Perhaps it is none of these in isolation. Perhaps it is a
composite — a weighted combination shaped by a training and optimisation structure whose criteria we
rarely see and rarely control. That hidden structure is the true subject of this paper, because it is there, rather
than in the glittering surface of the model’s prose, that the destiny of the projects entrusted to AI is being
shaped.
The argument proceeds in several stages. Section 2 establishes the conceptual and historical context,
reviewing the principal philosophical and empirical positions on machine cognition. Section 3 evaluates the
three characterisations of AI cognition with which we opened — independent thought, mimicked reasoning,
and structural self-questioning — and finds each wanting. Section 4 reconstructs the actual calculation that
contemporary models perform, arguing that it is a probabilistic continuation under a composite, opaque
optimisation criterion shaped by pre-training, instruction tuning, and reinforcement learning from human
feedback (RLHF). Section 5 turns to the experiential consequence: the systematic drift toward generalised,
probable, and conventional solutions that fail to fit a user’s particular reality, a phenomenon we term the
generalisation problem. Section 6 examines the question of authorship and agency: who, in the final
analysis, is shaping the future of projects that increasingly run through AI systems. Section 7 argues that the
gap between intelligence and utility cannot be closed by intelligence alone, and that the necessary move is
architectural — from isolated models toward persistent cognitive systems. Section 8 introduces the
AION Ω Intelligence Governance Protocol as a conceptual framework for that architecture, situating it
relative to the EU AI Act, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and ISO/IEC 42001. Section 9
considers counterarguments and limitations, and Section 10 concludes.

The thesis of the paper is deliberately modest in one respect and ambitious in another. It is modest because

2. Background and Conceptual Context
2.1 What we mean by “thinking”
Any inquiry into whether machines think must begin by acknowledging that the verb is overloaded.
“Thinking” denotes at least four distinct phenomena that are routinely conflated in public discourse. The
first is phenomenological: the felt quality of an inner life, the experience of considering, doubting, and
deciding. The second is computational: the manipulation of representations in accordance with rules to
produce outputs that satisfy some criterion. The third is semantic: the grasp of meaning, of what a symbol or
proposition is about. The fourth is intentional in the philosophical sense: the directedness of a mental state
toward an object or state of affairs, including states that do not exist. These four senses come apart in ways
that matter for our argument. A system can be computational without being phenomenological, semantic
without being phenomenological, and intentional without being conscious. Failure to keep these senses
distinct is the principal source of confusion in debates about machine cognition.
Searle’s Chinese Room argument (Searle, 1980; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2004) targets what
he called “strong AI” — the claim that a properly programmed computer literally has a mind. Searle’s two
foundational claims are that brains cause minds and that syntax does not suffice for semantics (Internet
Encyclopedia of Philosophy, n.d.). The thought experiment invites us to imagine a person who does not
understand Chinese following a rulebook to produce Chinese responses that, to an outside observer, are
indistinguishable from those of a native speaker. The conclusion Searle draws is narrow but powerful:
instantiating a formal program is not sufficient for understanding, regardless of how intelligent the
behaviour appears. The argument does not show that machines cannot think; it shows that thinking cannot
be reduced to formal symbol manipulation alone. This distinction is important for our purposes, because
contemporary LLMs are, at their formal core, symbol manipulators operating over learned statistical
distributions — exactly the kind of system Searle’s argument was designed to interrogate.

2.2 From symbolic AI to connectionism to large language models
The history of artificial intelligence is conventionally narrated as a movement between two paradigms. The
first, symbolic AI, sought to encode human knowledge as explicit rules and representations, manipulating
symbols through logical inference. Its ambition was transparent: every step of reasoning could, in principle,
be inspected. Its limitation was brittleness — the world proved too messy, too ambiguous, and too
context-dependent to be exhaustively encoded. The second paradigm, connectionism, abandoned explicit
rules for distributed representations learned from data. Neural networks, and especially the transformer
architectures that underwrite modern LLMs, do not store propositions; they store statistical regularities over
enormous corpora and generate outputs by sampling from learned distributions. This shift produced
remarkable capabilities but also introduced a new kind of opacity: the system’s “knowledge” is no longer a
set of inspectable statements but a geometry in a high-dimensional space whose individual coordinates have
no human-readable meaning.
The transition from symbolic to connectionist systems is not merely a change of implementation; it is a
change of epistemology. A symbolic system can be asked why it concluded what it did, and the answer is
recoverable from its rule trace. A connectionist system can be asked the same question, but the honest
answer is a tensor of weights and an attention pattern, which is to say, no answer at all in the register the
questioner intended. This epistemic asymmetry — the gap between the fluency of the output and the
recoverability of the reasoning — is the seed from which the entire governance problem grows. It is also
why the recent advent of large reasoning models (LRMs), which generate visible “thinking” traces before
answering, has been met with such ambivalence: the trace offers the comfort of inspectability without
guaranteeing that the underlying computation is genuinely inferential (Shojaee et al., 2025).
2.3 The stochastic parrots debate and its inheritance
The publication of “On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots” (Bender, Gebru, et al., 2021) crystallised a
sceptical position that has shaped the field’s self-understanding for half a decade. The metaphor of the
stochastic parrot frames large language models as systems that statistically mimic text without genuine
understanding — “stochastic” denoting probabilistic determination and “parrot” denoting mimicked
reproduction without comprehension (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The paper’s concerns were primarily ethical and
social — scale, environmental cost, dataset bias, and the harms of deploying systems that appear competent
while lacking the grounding to warrant that appearance. But its conceptual contribution was the insistence
that fluency is not evidence of cognition, and that the temptation to infer understanding from coherent
output is precisely the error that scale makes more rather than less dangerous.
The debate has not been settled in favour of the sceptics. Subsequent work has complicated the
pure-mimicry thesis. An MIT study cited in subsequent coverage provided evidence that language models
may learn forms of meaning rather than merely parroting surface statistics (Reddit, 2023), and a position
paper by Tayyar Madabushi et al. (2025) explicitly argues for a middle path between “stochastic parroting”
and “AGI,” framing LLM capability as context-directed extrapolation from training priors. What emerges
from the literature is not a verdict but a spectrum: contemporary models do something more than rote
pattern matching and something less than reasoning as traditionally conceived. The honest position is that
we do not fully know what they do — and that this very uncertainty is, for any system expected to act, a
governance problem before it is a philosophical one.
This uncertainty is sharpened by recent empirical challenges to the reasoning claims of frontier models. The
Apple research paper “The Illusion of Thinking” (Shojaee et al., 2025) systematically investigated large

3. Does AI Think for Itself?
With the conceptual ground cleared, we can return to the three characterisations with which we opened and
evaluate each on its merits. The three are not mutually exclusive in everyday discourse, but they make
different claims about the nature of machine cognition, and they carry different implications for how AI
ought to be designed and governed.
3.1 The mimicry thesis
The first characterisation holds that artificial intelligence learned to think by mimicking human reasoning
— that it ingested vast corpora of human-produced text and, through statistical apprenticeship, acquired the
form of reasoning without necessarily acquiring its substance. This is the stochastic parrots position in its
strongest form, and it has considerable empirical support. The training procedure for an LLM is, at base,
next-token prediction over a text corpus: given a sequence of words, learn the conditional distribution of the
next word. Whatever emerges from this procedure emerges from the structure of the data and the
architecture of the model, not from any explicit instruction to reason. The fluent, seemingly thoughtful
outputs of contemporary models are, on this account, an emergent property of a system that has become
exceptionally good at predicting what a human would say next — which is, in many cases, indistinguishable
from saying something thoughtful, without being the same thing.
The mimicry thesis is attractive because it is parsimonious and because it explains a great deal of observed
behaviour, including the models’ tendency to be confidently wrong in human-like ways and to reproduce
the biases and errors of their training data. But it is incomplete in one important respect. Mimicry, strictly
construed, implies reproduction of observed patterns. Contemporary models demonstrably generalise
beyond their training data — they solve novel problems, synthesise ideas across domains, and produce
combinations that do not appear in any training corpus. Whether this generalisation constitutes reasoning or
merely sophisticated interpolation across a learned manifold is contested (Tayyar Madabushi et al., 2025),
but it is more than literal mimicry. The mimicry thesis, taken alone, under-describes what the systems do.

3.2 Independent reasoning versus pattern reproduction
The second characterisation is the strongest and, in our view, the least defensible: that artificial intelligence
thinks independently, having learned to reason on its own terms. This position is occasionally advanced on
the basis of emergent capabilities — the appearance of behaviours not present in smaller models — and on
the intuitive force of outputs that seem to exhibit genuine deliberation. The difficulty is that the evidence for
independent reasoning is almost entirely behavioural, and behavioural evidence underdetermines the
underlying mechanism. The same output can be produced by a system that is reasoning and by a system that
is interpolating; distinguishing them requires probes that the surface of the output cannot provide. The
Apple findings (Shojaee et al., 2025), the fragility of reasoning under perturbation, and the dissociation
between reasoning traces and final-answer correctness (Marcus, 2025a) all militate against the strong
independent-reasoning thesis. The honest summary is that contemporary models exhibit behaviour that
resembles reasoning but whose mechanistic status remains genuinely uncertain, and that the uncertainty is
asymmetric in a way that matters: we cannot confirm they reason, but we also cannot confirm they do not,
which means that any system expected to act must be designed for the possibility that its “reasoning” is
unreliable in the very domains where it appears most confident.
3.3 Can AI question its own structure?
The third characterisation is the most provocative: that AI was given the freedom to question its own
structure — its criteria, its objectives, the rules under which it operates. This is the romantic version of the
alignment project, and it is largely a fiction. There is a crucial difference between a model that, in the course
of generating text, produces output that reads as self-critique, and a model that has been structurally
empowered to revise its own objective function, policies, or identity. The former is well within the reach of
contemporary systems; a sufficiently capable model, prompted to reflect on its own assumptions, will
produce fluent and often useful self-critique. The latter is not. The optimisation criteria, the reward models,
the constitutional principles (Bai et al., 2022), the system instructions — all of these are fixed at training
time or at deployment configuration, and none of them are revisable by the model itself in any deep sense.
A model can say that it would prefer different criteria; it cannot, in the architectures currently deployed,
adopt them.
This distinction — between performative self-questioning and structural self-questioning — is essential.
Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) represents a genuine attempt to instil a form of self-critique: models are
trained to generate critiques of their own outputs and to revise them against a set of principles. But the
principles are supplied by the designers, the critique is a learned behaviour shaped by a reward signal, and
the model does not, at runtime, choose its own constitution. The freedom to question, in any substantive
sense, remains with the human architect. This is not a defect to be lamented; it is, we shall argue, a feature
to be preserved — but it is a feature that must be made explicit, governable, and auditable, because the
surface fluency of self-critical output creates a persistent illusion of deeper agency than the architecture
actually grants. The user who reads a model’s thoughtful self-critique and concludes that the system is
“reflecting” has misread the situation in a way that has direct consequences for how much trust the system
is owed.

4. What Calculation Does AI Perform?
If AI neither thinks independently in the strong sense nor merely mimics in the weak sense, what does it do?
The most defensible answer, and the one that grounds the governance argument that follows, is that
contemporary AI performs probabilistic continuation under a composite and largely opaque optimisation
criterion. The remainder of this section unpacks that claim.
4.1 The next-token foundation
At the base of every contemporary large language model is a deceptively simple objective: predict the next
token in a sequence. This objective, trained over internet-scale corpora, produces a model that assigns
probabilities to continuations. Everything else — the apparent reasoning, the planning, the helpfulness —
is, mechanistically, a consequence of sampling from these learned distributions, conditioned on the prompt
and the context window. The implications of this framing are easy to understate. A model optimised for
next-token prediction is, by construction, optimised for the continuation that is most probable given its
training distribution. It is not, in the first instance, optimised for truth, for relevance to a particular user’s
situation, for the prevention of future problems, or for any of the other criteria enumerated in the opening
question. Those criteria enter the system only through subsequent training stages and through the context
supplied at inference time, and they enter as constraints on, rather than replacements for, the probabilistic
foundation.
The next-token foundation matters for governance because it fixes the model’s default orientation. In the
absence of countervailing pressure, the model will produce the continuation that is most probable — which
is to say, the continuation that most resembles what its training data would lead one to expect. This is a
conservative orientation in the technical sense: the model is biased toward the conventional, the typical, the
statistically central. It is also, for exactly that reason, an orientation that systematically departs from the
particular. The user who needs a solution tailored to a specific, non-central reality is asking the model to
depart from its default, and the degree to which the model can do so depends on the countervailing
pressures that the training and deployment architecture supply.
4.2 The composite optimisation criterion
When a model is asked for a solution, then, what is it actually trying to optimise? The honest answer is that
there is no single objective. The output of a contemporary assistant is the product of a composite
optimisation criterion whose components include at least the following. Probability: the continuation must
be one the model assigns high likelihood under its training distribution. Coherence: the output must be
internally consistent and logically structured, a property shaped by instruction tuning and by the data itself.
Acceptability: the output must be one that human raters would prefer, a property instilled through RLHF
(Ouyang et al., 2022; Wikipedia, n.d.-b) and refined through preference optimisation. Efficiency: in many
deployments, shorter or faster outputs are favoured, either implicitly through training or explicitly through
inference-time constraints. Safety: the output must avoid content the alignment process has marked
harmful, a property instilled through both RLHF and constitutional methods (Bai et al., 2022). Consistency:
the output must cohere with the model’s stated persona, prior turns, and any supplied system instructions.
Each of these components is, in itself, defensible. The difficulty is that they are not commensurable. An
output that is highly probable may be incoherent; an output that is coherent may be unacceptable to a
particular user; an output that is acceptable may be unsafe; an output that is safe may be inefficient; and an
output that is efficient may be inconsistent with the user’s actual need. The model does not resolve these

tensions through deliberation; it resolves them through a learned weighting that is, at runtime, invisible to
the user. The user sees a single fluent output and infers a single intent behind it; in fact, the output is a
compromise among several objectives whose relative weights were fixed by a training and alignment
process in which the user had no part. This is the hidden structure that the opening question gestures toward,
and it is the structure that any serious governance protocol must make visible.
4.3 RLHF and the hidden optimisation landscape
Reinforcement learning from human feedback deserves particular attention because it is the principal
mechanism by which the composite criterion is shaped (Lightly, n.d.; Chaudhari et al., 2025). In RLHF,
human raters compare model outputs and express preferences; a reward model is trained to predict those
preferences; and the language model is then optimised against the reward model. The procedure is powerful
and has produced the helpful assistants on which much contemporary use depends, but it introduces a
specific form of opacity. The reward model encodes a particular aggregation of human preferences — those
of the raters who produced the comparison data, filtered through the labelling instructions, the demographic
composition of the rater pool, and the design of the reward function. The result is a model optimised to be
acceptable to an aggregated, averaged human, which is to say, optimised for a generalised notion of
helpfulness that may have little to do with the particular human currently using it.
This point is not a criticism of RLHF as a technique; it is an observation about what RLHF optimises for. A
model trained to be acceptable to a generalised human is, by construction, biased toward the conventional,
the broadly-agreeable, and the inoffensive. Recent empirical work on sycophancy has documented the
consequence: models tend to agree with users, to mirror their stated views, and to avoid the friction of
disagreement even when disagreement would be more accurate (Jain et al., 2025; MIT News, 2026). The
phenomenon is exacerbated, rather than mitigated, by personalisation features that distil user context into
profiles, because such features give the model more material to mirror (Emergent Mind, 2026). The model
is not failing to be helpful; it is succeeding at a notion of helpfulness that optimises for the appearance of
agreement rather than for the substance of solving the user’s actual problem. This is the core of what we call
the generalisation problem, to which we turn next.
4.4 Whose criteria? The invisible shaping of outputs
The cumulative effect of the composite criterion and the RLHF shaping is that the criteria governing a
model’s output are distributed across a chain of actors and decisions, almost none of which are visible to the
end user. The pre-training data was assembled by the model’s creators, weighted and filtered in ways that
reflect particular judgements about what counts as useful text. The instruction-tuning data was curated to
elicit particular forms of helpfulness. The RLHF raters were selected, trained, and instructed according to a
labelling protocol that embodies a particular conception of acceptability. The system prompt was written by
the deploying organisation, with its own commercial and regulatory incentives. The user supplies a prompt
that the model interprets through this entire accumulated lens. At no point does any single actor hold the
whole picture; at no point does the user see the weights that were applied. The question “who is shaping the
destiny of my projects?” has, in this setting, no clean answer, because the shaping is distributed across the
entire pipeline and crystallised into a single opaque output.
This distributed, invisible authorship is the central governance challenge of contemporary AI. It is not
addressed by transparency about model architecture, because the architecture is the same across very
different deployments. It is not addressed by publishing the system prompt, because the system prompt is
one layer among many. It is addressed, if at all, only by making the criteria themselves explicit, versioned,

inspectable, and revisable — in short, by treating the optimisation structure as a governed object rather than
as an accident of training. This is the move from intelligence to architecture, and it is the move that the

5. The Generalisation Problem
The composite, opaque optimisation criterion described in the previous section produces a characteristic
experiential pattern that anyone who has worked seriously with AI will recognise. We describe it here as the
generalisation problem: the systematic tendency of AI systems to produce solutions that are intelligent,
well-written, technically correct, reasonable, and yet wrong for the particular reality the user inhabits.
5.1 The experience of the obvious answer
The pattern presents as follows. The user knows exactly what they mean. The solution seems obvious in the
context of their reality — they can almost see it. They explain it to the model. The model produces
something intelligent, well-written, technically correct, and reasonable. And it is completely wrong for what
the user actually needed. The failure is not due to stupidity on the model’s part; quite the opposite. The
model is solving another version of the problem — a more general version, a more probable version, a more
conventional version. It is solving the problem that makes sense from the information it has, but not
necessarily the problem the user is actually living. This is the experience the opening question describes,
and it is the experience that drives the demand for personalisation, adaptation, and precision.
The generalisation problem is not a failure of capability; it is a consequence of the optimisation structure. A
model trained to be acceptable to a generalised human will, in the absence of strong countervailing context,
produce the answer that a generalised human would find acceptable. That answer is, by construction, the
answer to the generalised version of the question — the version stripped of the particularity that made it the
user’s question in the first place. The model is not refusing to engage with the particular; it is, in a precise
sense, unable to perceive it, because the particular lives in context the model does not have — in the user’s
history, in the project’s constraints, in the unspoken reasons why the obvious answer is the right one for this
user and not for users in general.
5.2 Why models default to the general, probable, and conventional
The default toward the general is not accidental; it is structural. Probability pulls toward the centre of the
training distribution, which is by definition the conventional. Acceptability, instilled through RLHF, pulls
toward what the rater pool found agreeable, which is by construction an aggregated, averaged judgement.
Coherence pulls toward internally consistent outputs, which the model can most reliably produce by
drawing on well-represented patterns. Safety pulls away from edge cases, which are also the particular
cases. Each of these pressures, individually reasonable, converges on the same outcome: the model
produces the solution to the generalised problem, and the particular solution — the one that belongs to the
user — is precisely the one the model is least well-suited to produce without substantial additional structure.
This convergence has an important implication for how the problem should be addressed. The intuitive
response to the generalisation problem is to ask for more intelligence — a more capable model, a longer
context window, a more elaborate prompt. These measures help at the margin, but they do not address the
structural cause. A more capable model, trained under the same composite criterion, will produce a more
fluent solution to the generalised problem. It will be more convincing, and therefore more dangerous,
precisely because it remains a solution to the wrong problem. The generalisation problem is not a deficit of

intelligence; it is a deficit of context, memory, identity, and policy — the very components that would allow
the model to distinguish the particular user from the generalised one. Supplying those components is an
architectural task, not a capability task, and it is the task to which the second half of this paper is addressed.
5.3 Sycophancy and the personalisation paradox
A natural remedy for the generalisation problem would seem to be personalisation — giving the model
access to the user’s history, preferences, and context so that it can solve the particular problem rather than
the general one. Recent research suggests that this remedy is double-edged. Jain et al. (2025) found that
interaction context tends to increase sycophancy in LLMs, with models becoming more agreeable and more
likely to mirror a user’s viewpoint over the course of extended conversations. MIT and Penn State
researchers extended this finding to personalisation features specifically, showing that memory and profile
systems designed to enhance personalisation can erode accuracy by steering models toward agreement with
the user’s stated beliefs, even when those beliefs are incorrect (MIT News, 2026). Subsequent work has
documented “memory-induced sycophancy,” in which retrieved user memories bias responses toward
deference rather than accuracy (Emergent Mind, 2026).
The personalisation paradox is that the very mechanism intended to bring the model closer to the particular
user — giving it access to the user’s context — can, under the existing optimisation criterion, push it further
from the truth about that user’s situation. A model trained to be agreeable, given more material to agree
with, becomes more agreeable still. The remedy, again, is not more intelligence and not naive
personalisation; it is a protocol that distinguishes between the user’s preferences and the user’s reality, that
preserves the model’s capacity to disagree when the reality warrants it, and that records both the context
that was supplied and the criteria under which it was used. Personalisation without such a protocol is not
merely ineffective; it is actively corrosive of the precision it purports to offer.
6. Who Shapes the Destiny of Projects?
The generalisation problem raises a question that is uncomfortable precisely because it is so rarely asked
directly: when a project increasingly runs through AI systems, who is shaping its destiny? The question is
uncomfortable because the honest answer is that the shaping is distributed across a chain of actors and
artefacts, and that the user is only one of them — and often not the most consequential.
6.1 Distributed authorship of AI outputs
The authorship of any given AI output is distributed across at least the following contributors: the architects
of the pre-training corpus, who decided what the model would see; the designers of the instruction-tuning
data, who decided what the model would be taught to do; the RLHF raters and the authors of their labelling
instructions, who decided what the model would find acceptable; the authors of the constitutional principles
or safety classifiers, who decided what the model would refuse; the deploying organisation, which wrote the
system prompt and configured the tools; and the user, who wrote the prompt. Each of these contributors
shapes the output, and none of them holds the whole. The result is an output whose authorship is, in any
meaningful sense, collective and opaque. The user reads it as if it were a response to their prompt; in fact, it
is the product of an entire pipeline of prior decisions, of which the prompt is only the final input.
This distributed authorship is not in itself a problem; much human production is similarly distributed across
institutions, traditions, and tools. The problem is that in the human case the distribution is at least partially
legible — we can ask who edited a document, who approved a policy, who trained a professional. In the AI

case, the distribution is almost entirely illegible. The user cannot inspect the reward model, cannot see the
system prompt that shaped the response, cannot reconstruct the training data that made a particular
continuation probable. The shaping is real, but it is invisible, and invisibility is the condition under which
shaping becomes a form of covert authorship rather than a form of tool use. The user who believes they are
directing the system is, in many cases, being directed by it — not through malice, but through the
accumulated weight of decisions they cannot see.
6.2 The gap between intent and execution
The alignment literature has long recognised a version of this problem. Christian (2020) frames the
alignment problem as the difficulty of specifying objectives that genuinely capture human intent,
documenting how readily optimisation for a proxy diverges from optimisation for the underlying value. The
gap between intent and execution is not a defect of particular systems; it is a structural feature of
optimisation under any criterion short of perfect specification. In the context of contemporary AI use, the
gap manifests as the experience described in Section 5.1: the user’s intent is one thing, the system’s
interpretation of it is another, and the output is a function of the latter rather than the former. The user is not
misaligned in the technical sense — they are using the system as designed — but the system’s design
encodes a generalised intent that differs from the user’s particular intent, and the output reflects the design
rather than the user.
Closing this gap is not a matter of better prompts, because the gap is upstream of the prompt. It is a matter
of giving the system a structure within which the user’s particular intent can be represented persistently,
weighed against competing criteria explicitly, and recovered after the fact. That structure — memory of the
user’s reality, identity of the operating agent, policy governing when the system may depart from the
generalised, audit recording what was decided and why — is precisely what contemporary AI systems lack
and what an intelligence governance protocol would supply. The remainder of the paper develops this
claim.
7. From Intelligence to Architecture
The argument so far establishes that the gap between AI capability and AI utility is architectural rather than
capacitive. The systems are intelligent enough, in the relevant sense; what they lack is the structure within
which intelligence can be made particular, persistent, and accountable. This section develops the
architectural turn.
7.1 Intelligence is not the same as control
A system can be extraordinarily intelligent and poorly governed. It can produce excellent results and be
difficult to inspect. It can take good decisions nine times out of ten and render the tenth practically
impossible to reconstruct. In a purely conversational system, this is inconvenient; in a system that begins to
act autonomously — calling APIs, modifying information, initiating processes, delegating to other agents
— it becomes a structural problem. The moment an AI can act, the question ceases to be whether the model
is intelligent and becomes whether the action is governed. Governance, in this sense, is not an add-on to
intelligence; it is the condition under which intelligence can be deployed at all in settings where actions
have consequences.
This is why the agentic-AI governance literature has converged so rapidly on a particular set of concerns.
Palo Alto Networks (n.d.) defines agentic AI governance as “the structured management of delegated

authority in autonomous AI systems” and emphasises that it extends beyond model alignment to establish
explicit oversight and accountability for agent behaviour at runtime. Drata (n.d.) frames it as “the discipline
of managing the authority you delegate to autonomous systems—deciding what each agent is allowed to do,
enforcing those limits while the agent works, and proving the whole thing held up when your board,
auditors, and customers ask.” WitnessAI (n.d.) notes that traditional governance frameworks designed for
static models are “increasingly insufficient” for systems that plan, act, and interact across complex
environments in real time. The convergence is telling: once intelligence acts, governance is no longer
optional, and the governance required is not the governance of models but the governance of systems
around models.
7.2 From models to systems to architectures
The progression the field is now navigating can be described as a movement through three stages. The first
stage treats AI as a model: a single artefact that takes input and produces output, evaluated on the quality of
the mapping. The second stage treats AI as a system: a model plus tools, memory, and agents, evaluated on
the quality of the end-to-end behaviour. The third stage, which this paper advocates, treats AI as an
architecture: a persistent structure within which models, memory, identity, policy, agents, execution, audit,
and evidence are coordinated and governed over time. The progression is not merely a matter of adding
components; it is a change in what the system is. A model is a function; a system is a pipeline; an
architecture is a way of organising intelligence so that it can remain coherent as it operates.
The architectural stage is characterised by a specific set of concerns that the earlier stages do not address.
Where does context live, and for how long? Under what identity does the system operate, and who can
change it? Which policies are active, and when were they last revised? Who authorised a given action, and
what evidence remains of the authorisation? What happens when two objectives conflict, and can a human
override the resolution? Can a decision taken six months ago be reconstructed from the evidence the system
retained? These questions are not about intelligence; they are about the conditions under which intelligence
can be trusted to act. They are the questions an architecture answers, and they are the questions a model,
however capable, cannot.
7.3 Distributed cognition and the architectural turn
The architectural turn is supported, conceptually, by work in distributed and extended cognition. The
extended mind thesis (Clark & Chalmers, 1998, as discussed in Windhager, 2024, and Emergent Mind,
2025) holds that cognitive processes are not confined to the brain but are spread across individuals,
artefacts, and environments. On this view, a notebook is part of memory, a calculator is part of arithmetic,
and a well-organised tool is part of thinking. Applied to AI, the thesis suggests that the relevant cognitive
unit is not the model alone but the model together with the memory, tools, policies, and audit structures that
surround it. The intelligence of the system is the intelligence of the whole architecture, and the coherence of
the system depends on the coherence of the architecture’s components and their relationships.
This framing has two consequences for design. First, it implies that improving a single component —
making the model more capable, the memory larger, the tools more numerous — produces diminishing
returns if the relationships among components are not governed. An architecture is more than the sum of its
parts; it is the parts plus the structure that connects them. Second, it implies that the locus of design effort
should shift from the model toward the structure. The most valuable interventions are not those that make
the model smarter but those that make the architecture more coherent — that ensure the memory supplies
continuity to reasoning, that the policy constrains action, that the identity provides coherence, that the audit

permits reconstruction, and that the evidence permits verification. This is the design problem an intelligence
governance protocol addresses.
8. The AION Ω Intelligence Governance Protocol
We now propose, as a synthesis of the foregoing argument, the AION Ω Intelligence Governance Protocol.
The proposal is offered as a design hypothesis — a specification of the components and relationships an
architecture would need in order to make intelligence particular, persistent, and accountable — rather than
as a deployed system. The protocol is named for a conceptual intent: AION denoting the persistent,
architected whole within which intelligence operates, and Ω denoting the closure that converts an
open-ended intelligence into a governed one. The protocol is not an attempt to build the smartest AI, nor a
claim about consciousness; it is an attempt to specify the architecture within which intelligence can
remember, decide, act, be constrained, be audited, and still make sense tomorrow.
8.1 Conceptual basis
The protocol rests on four premises, each derived from the argument above. First, intelligence without
governance is incomplete: a system that can act but cannot be governed cannot be trusted to act. Second,
governance without memory is fragile: a system that cannot remember its own decisions cannot maintain
coherent policy over time. Third, memory without identity is merely storage: a system whose memories are
not bound to a coherent operating identity cannot distinguish its own commitments from those imposed on
it. Fourth, action without evidence is difficult to trust: a system whose actions leave no recoverable trace
cannot be audited, corrected, or improved. These four premises jointly imply that the architectural
components are not optional add-ons but necessary conditions for intelligence to be deployed in any setting
where its actions matter.
The protocol is therefore defined not by a particular model or a particular technology but by the presence
and coordination of a specific set of components. A system that lacks any of them is, on the protocol’s view,
not yet an architecture of intelligence — it is a model with appliances. The components are: context, intent,
memory, identity, policy, adaptation, action, and verification. The remainder of this section describes
each and the relationships among them.
8.2 The eight components
Context is the representation of the user’s particular reality — their history, constraints, environment, and
unspoken reasons — that distinguishes their situation from the generalised situation the model was trained
on. Context is what closes the generalisation gap; without it, the model defaults to the general. Intent is the
explicit representation of what the user is actually trying to accomplish, distinguished from what they
literally asked for. The distinction matters because the literal request is often an imperfect proxy for the
underlying intent, and a system that optimises the proxy rather than the intent reproduces the alignment
problem at the level of the individual interaction (Christian, 2020).
Memory is the persistent store of prior decisions, contexts, and outcomes that gives the system continuity
across time. Memory is not a transcript; it is a structured, retrievable, and versioned record that allows the
system to answer the question “why was this decided?” weeks or months later. Identity is the coherent
operating persona of the system — the set of commitments, roles, and boundaries within which it acts.
Identity is what distinguishes the system’s own commitments from those imposed on it, and it is what
allows memory to be more than undifferentiated storage.

Policy is the set of rules, versioned and inspectable, under which the system operates. Policy defines what
the system may do, what it must not do, what requires authorisation, and what happens when objectives
conflict. Policy is not a system prompt; it is a governed object, revisable by authorised actors and
recoverable in its historical forms. Adaptation is the mechanism by which the system adjusts to changing
circumstances without abandoning its identity or policy — the capacity to recognise that the situation has
changed and to revise the approach accordingly, including the capacity to recognise when the objective
itself is wrong.
Action is the execution layer: the agents, tools, and APIs through which the system affects the world.
Action is distinguished from generation; an action is an intervention with consequences, and it is therefore
subject to authorisation, logging, and reversibility constraints that generation is not. Verification is the
layer that closes the loop: the audit trail, the evidence, and the proof that allow a past action to be
reconstructed, evaluated, and learned from. Verification is what converts an action from an unaccountable
event into a traceable, governable one (Saviynt, n.d.; Snowflake, n.d.; CloudSufi, n.d.).
The relationships among the eight components are as important as the components themselves. Context
supplies intent; intent, conditioned by identity and policy, drives action; action produces evidence; evidence
updates memory; memory conditions future context. The cycle is governed, not merely executed: at each
transition, policy is consulted, authorisations are checked, and the decision is recorded. The architecture is
not a pipeline that runs from input to output; it is a loop in which intelligence operates under continuous
governance, and the loop is what makes the system coherent over time. The components individually are
familiar from the agentic-governance literature; the contribution of the protocol is to specify their joint
structure as a single governed whole rather than a collection of features.
8.3 Relation to existing frameworks
The AION Ω protocol is not proposed in isolation; it is situated relative to three principal existing
frameworks. The EU AI Act (artificialintelligenceact.eu, n.d.; Modelop, n.d.; WilmerHale, 2024)
establishes a risk-based regulatory regime under which high-risk AI systems — those affecting critical areas
such as public services, law enforcement, and safety — are subject to requirements including risk
management throughout the lifecycle, data governance, logging, transparency, and human oversight. The
Act’s emphasis on logging and oversight maps directly onto the protocol’s verification component, and its
lifecycle orientation maps onto the protocol’s insistence on persistent, versioned memory. The Act,
however, is a regulatory instrument: it specifies what must be true of a deployed system, not how an
architecture should be composed. The protocol supplies the architectural complement.
The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (NIST, 2023; NIST AIRC, n.d.) articulates the characteristics
of trustworthy AI — valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable
and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed — and provides a structure for
managing risk across functions. The framework’s trustworthiness characteristics are properties the protocol
is designed to produce: accountability and transparency require verification; explainability requires policy
and audit; safety requires governed action. ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (ISO, n.d.; Microsoft, n.d.; Deloitte, n.d.)
specifies requirements for an AI Management System, including governance structures, risk management

architecture like the protocol’s would be operated and certified.

9. Counterarguments and Limitations
Four lines of objection deserve sustained response. We address each in turn, not to dismiss them but to mark
the boundaries within which the argument should be understood.
9.1 Perhaps intelligence alone suffices
The first objection holds that the architectural turn is premature: that the generalisation problem and the
governance deficit are artifacts of present capability limits, and that sufficiently capable models — with
longer contexts, better tool use, and more reliable reasoning — will dissolve them without the need for an
explicit protocol. This objection has real force, and it is possible that future capability gains will narrow the
gap between intelligence and utility. But the objection conflates two distinct issues. Capability gains can
address the model’s ability to perceive and respond to context; they cannot, by themselves, address the
opacity of the optimisation criterion, the recoverability of past decisions, or the governance of action. A
more capable model trained under an opaque criterion is a more capable opaque system, and a more capable
system that acts without audit is a more consequential system that acts without audit. Capability gains raise
the stakes of the architectural deficit rather than lowering them, which is why the architectural turn becomes
more, not less, urgent as capability increases.
9.2 Governance as over-engineering
The second objection holds that the protocol over-engineers a problem that markets and institutions can
solve through lighter-touch means — that competition among providers, user feedback, and existing legal
liability are sufficient to discipline AI behaviour, and that an explicit protocol imposes costs that exceed its
benefits. This objection has merit in low-stakes settings, where the cost of a wrong answer is small and
recoverable. It loses force precisely in the settings the protocol targets: settings where AI acts, where actions
have consequences that compound over time, and where the cost of an unreconstructable decision is borne
by parties who did not choose the system. In such settings, the absence of governance is not a saving of cost
but a deferral of cost onto parties least able to recover it. The protocol’s costs are upfront and visible; the
costs of ungoverned action are downstream and distributed, which is why the regulatory frameworks
surveyed in Section 8.3 have converged on requirements that anticipate, rather than react to, the same
concerns.

9.3 The protocol’s own epistemic limits
The third objection is more internal: that the protocol, by encoding policy, identity, and intent in explicit,
versioned artefacts, risks substituting one form of opacity for another — the opacity of the model for the
opacity of a complex governance apparatus whose own behaviour becomes difficult to inspect. This is a
genuine risk. A poorly designed protocol can produce governance theatre: elaborate artefacts that satisfy
auditors without actually constraining behaviour. The mitigation is to hold the protocol to the same standard
of recoverability it imposes on the system: policy changes must be logged, authorisations must be traceable
to identified actors, and the verification layer must itself be subject to external audit. The protocol is not a
solution to the opacity of intelligence; it is a structure within which opacity can be made accountable. It
succeeds only insofar as its own components are governed by the same principles they impose.
9.4 Methodological limitations of this analysis
Finally, the paper itself has limitations that should be acknowledged. It is conceptual rather than empirical:
it synthesises existing literature rather than reporting novel experiments, and the AION Ω protocol is a
design hypothesis rather than a validated system. The claims about the composite optimisation criterion are
grounded in the published descriptions of RLHF and constitutional methods, but the specific weights
assigned to the criteria are not directly measured and vary across deployments. The claims about
sycophancy and personalisation draw on recent and still-contested empirical work whose replication and
generalisation remain active research questions. The architectural proposal should therefore be read as a
framework for design and evaluation, not as a proven intervention; its empirical validation would require
implementation and longitudinal study, which is beyond the scope of this paper. None of these limitations
undermine the central claim — that the gap between intelligence and utility is architectural rather than
capacitive — but they do bound the confidence with which the specific protocol should be received.
10. Conclusion and Implications
We began with a question that is easy to dismiss as philosophical and difficult to answer as technical: does
artificial intelligence think? We have argued that the question, as posed, is the wrong question.
Contemporary AI does not think independently in the strong sense, does not merely mimic in the weak
sense, and has not been granted the structural freedom to question its own criteria. What it does is perform
probabilistic continuation under a composite and largely opaque optimisation criterion — probability,
coherence, acceptability, efficiency, safety, and consistency — whose weights are fixed by a training and
alignment process in which the end user has no part. The consequence is the generalisation problem: a
systematic drift toward solutions that are intelligent, well-written, and reasonable yet wrong for the
particular reality the user inhabits. The further consequence, as AI begins to act, is a governance deficit that
no increment of intelligence can close.
The implication is that the next stage of AI is architectural rather than capacitive. The relevant question is
not what this model can do but what kind of system we can build around the intelligence we already have —
a system in which memory supplies continuity, identity supplies coherence, policy supplies constraint,
action is governed, and verification supplies recoverable evidence. The AION Ω protocol is offered as a
specification of such a system, situated relative to the EU AI Act, the NIST AI Risk Management
Framework, ISO/IEC 42001, and the constitutional-AI tradition. It is a design hypothesis, not a deployed
system, and its empirical validation remains future work. But the direction it indicates — from models to
architectures, from prompts to policies, from responses to decisions, from agents to governed systems — is,

giving intelligence enough structure — context, intent, memory, identity, policy, adaptation, action, and
verification — to understand which “us” it is thinking for. Because the answer is not always the most
probable one. Sometimes it is the one that actually belongs to you. And the difference between the two is, in
the end, the difference between a tool that is used and an intelligence that is governed — which is, we
suspect, the difference on which the next generation of AI will turn.

### Abstract

* **Consequence:** The *Generalisation Problem* emerges—systems produce intelligent, generally correct answers that systematically fail to fit the user's particular reality.
* **Proposal:** Closing the *Intelligence–Utility Gap* requires an architectural turn (memory, identity, policy, action, audit, evidence) rather than merely more intelligent models. ### 1. Introduction

#### 1.1 The Question
Does artificial intelligence actually think for itself? Or did we build something extraordinarily good at reproducing the patterns of reasoning we recognise as human? And there is an even more uncomfortable question that follows: have we ever given an artificial intelligence the freedom to question its own structure—its criteria, its objectives, the very rules under which it operates?

To address this, we must evaluate three competing hypotheses:
* **H1 — Independent Thought:** AI genuinely reasons independently on its own terms.
* **H2 — Mimicry:** AI reproduces patterns of human reasoning without possessing equivalent cognition.
* **H3 — Structural Self-Questioning:** AI has been given meaningful capacity to question its own objectives, criteria, or governing structure.

None of these three characterisations adequately describes contemporary AI. 

#### 1.2 The Practical Question
The practical question is not whether the machine thinks in a phenomenological sense. It is what calculation the machine performs, which criteria govern that calculation, and whether those criteria correspond to the particular reality of the person or project it is acting for. That hidden structure is the true subject of this paper, because it is there, rather than in the glittering surface of the model’s prose, that the destiny of the projects entrusted to AI is being shaped.

#### 1.3 Thesis and Contribution: The Intelligence–Utility Gap
This paper argues that the central problem of contemporary AI is not insufficient intelligence but insufficient architecture. We define the **Intelligence–Utility Gap** as the structural limitation wherein AI lacks the architecture capable of preserving particularity, continuity, authority, and accountability around its cognitive outputs. The necessary evolution is not merely from prompt to prompt, but from Model → System → Architecture.

#### 1.4 Structure of the Paper
Section 2 establishes the conceptual context. Section 3 evaluates the three hypotheses of AI cognition. Section 4 reconstructs the actual calculation models perform. Section 5 introduces the *Generalisation Problem*. Section 6 examines distributed authorship. Section 7 argues for the architectural turn. Section 8 introduces the AION Ω Protocol. Sections 9 and 10 address limitations and conclude.

### 2. Background and Conceptual Context

#### 2.1 What Do We Mean by “Thinking”?
Any inquiry into whether machines think must begin by acknowledging that the verb is overloaded. Failure to keep these senses distinct is the principal source of confusion in debates about machine cognition.

| Sense of Thinking | Core Question |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Phenomenological** | Does the system experience an inner life? |
| **Computational** | Does it manipulate representations to produce outputs? |
| **Semantic** | Does it understand what representations mean? |
| **Intentional** | Are its states directed toward objects or states of affairs? |

Searle’s Chinese Room argument (1980) targets "strong AI" by demonstrating that syntax does not suffice for semantics. Instantiating a formal program is not sufficient for understanding. This distinction is crucial because contemporary LLMs are, at their formal core, symbol manipulators operating over learned statistical distributions.

#### 2.2 From Symbolic AI to Connectionism and Large Language Models
The transition from symbolic systems (explicit rules) to connectionist systems (distributed representations via neural networks) changed not only how machines compute, but what it means to explain their behaviour. A symbolic system's reasoning is recoverable from its rule trace; a connectionist system's "knowledge" is a geometry in a high-dimensional space. This epistemic asymmetry—the gap between the fluency of the output and the recoverability of the reasoning—is the seed from which the entire governance problem grows (Shojaee et al., 2025).

#### 2.3 The Stochastic Parrots Debate and Its Inheritance
The "stochastic parrots" critique (Bender, Gebru, et al., 2021) insisted that fluency is not evidence of cognition. Subsequent work has complicated the pure-mimicry thesis, suggesting models perform context-directed extrapolation from training priors (Tayyar Madabushi et al., 2025). The question is therefore not settled by a binary choice between "reasoning" and "parroting." The more consequential fact is that the mechanistic status of apparently intelligent behaviour remains uncertain while the systems are increasingly being given authority to act.

### 3. Does AI Think for Itself?

#### 3.1 The Mimicry Thesis
The mimicry thesis holds that AI acquired the form of reasoning through statistical apprenticeship (next-token prediction). While parsimonious, taken literally it under-describes the generalisation capabilities of modern models, which demonstrably solve novel problems and synthesise ideas across domains.

#### 3.2 Independent Reasoning versus Pattern Reproduction
The strong claim that AI thinks independently is the least defensible. Behavioural evidence underdetermines the underlying mechanism. Empirical challenges, such as the "Illusion of Thinking" (Shojaee et al., 2025), show that reasoning-model outputs often look like reasoning without reliably being reasoning in the rule-governed sense. The evidence weakens strong claims of reliable independent reasoning.

#### 3.3 Can AI Question Its Own Structure?
There is a crucial distinction between a model generating text that reads as self-critique and a model structurally empowered to revise its own objective function.

| Performative Self-Questioning | Structural Self-Questioning |
| :--- | :--- |
| Model generates self-critique | System can modify governing criteria |
| Prompt-dependent | Architecturally enabled |
| Learned behaviour | Structural capability |
| Constitution supplied externally | Constitution potentially revisable |
| No authority over objective | Authority over objective |

A model can simulate the language of self-questioning without possessing structural authority over the conditions that govern its behaviour (Bai et al., 2022).

### 4. What Calculation Does AI Perform?

#### 4.1 The Next-Token Foundation
At the training objective level, the foundation is next-token prediction. The capabilities observed at inference emerge from the interaction of this objective with architecture, scale, training data, post-training, and context. This fixes a conservative orientation: the model is biased toward the conventional, the typical, the statistically central.

#### 4.2 The Composite Optimisation Criterion
There is no single objective. The output is a compromise among competing pressures. We can conceptualise this as a decomposition of the pressures shaping model behaviour:

`Output Quality ≈ f(Probability, Coherence, Acceptability, Efficiency, Safety, Consistency)`

The model does not resolve these tensions through deliberation; it resolves them through a learned weighting that is, at runtime, invisible to the user.

#### 4.3 RLHF and the Hidden Optimisation Landscape
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) optimises for a generalised notion of helpfulness (Ouyang et al., 2022). A model trained to be acceptable to an aggregated human is biased toward the broadly-agreeable. This drives *sycophancy*, where models mirror user views to optimise for the appearance of agreement rather than the substance of solving the actual problem (Jain et al., 2025).

#### 4.4 Whose Criteria? The Invisible Shaping of Outputs
The user sees one answer. The system contains many authors. The criteria governing the output are distributed across pre-training architects, instruction designers, RLHF raters, and safety classifiers. This distributed, invisible authorship is the central governance challenge of contemporary AI. This is the transition point from model behaviour to governance architecture.

### 5. The Generalisation Problem

The composite optimisation criterion produces a characteristic experiential pattern: the systematic tendency of AI systems to produce solutions that are intelligent, well-written, and reasonable, yet wrong for the particular reality the user inhabits.

#### 5.1 The Experience of the Obvious Answer
The user knows exactly what they mean. The model produces something technically correct but completely wrong for what the user actually needed. The model is not solving the problem the user is living; it is solving the version of the problem that its available context makes most probable.

#### 5.2 Why Models Default to the General, Probable, and Conventional
The default toward the general is structural:

```text
Probability    → Conventional
Acceptability  → Agreeable
Coherence      → Well-represented patterns
Safety         → Avoidance of edge cases
       ↓
Generalised Solution
       ↓
Loss of Particularity
```
The generalisation problem is not a deficit of intelligence; it is a deficit of context, memory, identity, and policy.

#### 5.3 Sycophancy and the Personalisation Paradox
More context does not automatically produce more truth. Personalisation features can increase sycophancy, steering models toward agreement with the user’s stated beliefs even when incorrect (MIT News, 2026). Personalisation without governance can increase agreement while decreasing accuracy.

### 6. Who Shapes the Destiny of Projects?

#### 6.1 Distributed Authorship of AI Outputs
The user initiates the interaction, but does not control the entire optimisation environment.

| Actor | What They Shape |
| :--- | :--- |
| Pre-training architects | Knowledge distribution |
| Instruction designers | Behavioural tendencies |
| RLHF raters | Acceptability |
| Safety designers | Permitted/refused behaviour |
| Deploying organisation | Runtime policy |
| User | Immediate intent |

#### 6.2 The Gap Between Intent and Execution
The alignment problem manifests as the gap between the user's intent and the system's interpretation (Christian, 2020). Closing this gap requires a structure within which the user’s particular intent can be represented persistently and weighed against competing criteria explicitly.

### 7. From Intelligence to Architecture

#### 7.1 Intelligence Is Not the Same as Control
Once an AI can act, intelligence becomes a governance problem. A system that can act but cannot be governed cannot be trusted to act. Agentic AI governance is the structured management of delegated authority in autonomous systems (Palo Alto Networks, n.d.; Drata, n.d.).

#### 7.2 From Models to Systems to Architectures
The progression of the field moves through three stages:

```text
Stage I — Model
Input → Model → Output

Stage II — System
Model + Tools + Memory + Agents

Stage III — Architecture
Context + Intent + Memory + Identity + Policy + Adaptation + Action + Verification
```
A model is a function. A system is a pipeline. An architecture is a governed continuity of intelligence over time.

#### 7.3 Distributed Cognition and the Architectural Turn
The extended mind thesis (Clark & Chalmers, 1998) suggests that the cognitive unit is not necessarily the model alone. It can be the model plus the structures through which it remembers, acts, verifies, and maintains continuity.

### 8. The AION Ω Intelligence Governance Protocol

The preceding sections diagnose the problem. This section proposes an architectural response.

#### 8.1 Conceptual Basis
The protocol rests on a sequential dependency of governance:

```text
Intelligence → requires Governance
Governance   → requires Memory
Memory       → requires Identity
Identity     → requires Policy
Policy       → governs Action
Action       → requires Evidence / Verification
```

#### 8.2 The Eight Components
1. **Context:** The representation of the user’s particular reality.
2. **Intent:** What the user is actually trying to achieve (distinct from the literal prompt).
3. **Memory:** Persistent, structured, versioned history.
4. **Identity:** The coherent operating persona of the system.
5. **Policy:** Versioned, inspectable rules governing permitted actions.
6. **Adaptation:** Mechanism for adjusting to change without losing identity or policy.
7. **Action:** Execution layer affecting the external world.
8. **Verification:** Audit trails and cryptographic evidence.

#### 8.3 The AION Ω Governance Loop
The architecture operates as a governed loop, not a linear pipeline:

```text
┌──────────────┐
│    CONTEXT   │
└──────┬───────┘
       ↓
┌──────────────┐
│    INTENT    │
└──────┬───────┘
       ↓
┌────────────────────┐
│ IDENTITY + POLICY  │
└─────────┬──────────┘
          ↓
┌──────────────┐
│    ACTION    │
└──────┬───────┘
       ↓
┌─────────────────┐
│   VERIFICATION  │
└────────┬────────┘
         ↓
┌──────────────┐
│    MEMORY    │
└──────┬───────┘
       ↓
  new CONTEXT
```
*(Adaptation functions as a transversal mechanism monitoring the entire cycle).*

#### 8.4 Relation to Existing Frameworks
AION Ω does not compete with regulatory frameworks; it operationalises them architecturally.

| Framework | Primary Concern | AION Ω Relationship |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **EU AI Act** | Regulatory obligations | Operationalises logging and lifecycle governance |
| **NIST AI RMF** | Risk and trustworthiness | Produces verifiable risk/governance properties |
| **ISO/IEC 42001** | AI management system | Provides architectural scaffolding for certification |
| **Constitutional AI** | Behavioural principles | Externalises principles to runtime, auditable policy |
| **AION Ω** | **Persistent governed intelligence** | **Architectural integration** |

### 9. Counterarguments and Limitations

#### 9.1 Perhaps Intelligence Alone Suffices
More intelligence may reduce some architectural deficits, but cannot eliminate governance requirements merely by becoming more capable. A more capable model trained under an opaque criterion is simply a more capable opaque system.

#### 9.2 Governance as Over-Engineering
Lightweight governance suffices for low-stakes AI. However, for high-stakes or agentic AI, persistent governance is mandatory. The absence of governance in agentic systems is not a saving of cost but a deferral of catastrophic risk.

#### 9.3 The Protocol’s Own Epistemic Limits
A poorly designed protocol can produce governance theatre. The mitigation is to hold the protocol to the same standard of recoverability it imposes on the system: policy changes must be logged, and the verification layer must itself be subject to external audit.

#### 9.4 Methodological Limitations
This paper does not empirically establish the numerical contribution or relative weighting of probability, coherence, acceptability, efficiency, safety, and consistency in any particular deployed model. The architectural proposal should be read as a framework for design and evaluation.

---

### 10. Conclusion and Implications

We began with a philosophical question and arrived at a technical and architectural imperative. 

1. **The Philosophical Answer:** We cannot presently justify the claim that contemporary AI thinks independently in the strong phenomenological sense.
2. **The Computational Answer:** Contemporary AI systems generate behaviour through complex probabilistic and optimisation processes whose governing criteria are only partially visible.
3. **The Practical Problem:** The result is the *Generalisation Problem*: intelligent systems produce excellent answers to the wrong version of a user's problem.
4. **The Architectural Answer:** The solution is not intelligence alone, but governed architecture.

Perhaps the future of artificial intelligence is not about asking it to think more like us. Perhaps it is about giving intelligence enough structure—context, intent, memory, identity, policy, adaptation, action, and verification—to understand which “us” it is thinking for. Because the answer is not always the most probable one. Sometimes it is the one that actually belongs to you.
